How Wellness Founders Should Handle Client Testimonials

Every wellness founder knows testimonials work. Almost none of them have a system for getting good ones. Here's how we capture client stories that actually move people — without the cringe, the coercion, or the HIPAA headache.

If you sell wellness — a practice, a gym, a med spa, a coaching program — your client testimonials are the single most persuasive content you own. A stranger's doubt dissolves faster watching one of your real clients than it ever will reading your bio. We've seen it in the numbers across the brands we produce for: a two-minute client story outperforms a polished brand video almost every time.

And yet most wellness founders have a folder of nothing. A few screenshotted Google reviews. Maybe a text someone sent that they never asked permission to use. That's not a testimonial strategy. That's luck.

So let's fix it. Here's how we actually build a testimonial engine for the wellness brands we work with.

Why written testimonials quietly underperform

A five-star review is social proof. A filmed client story is a sales pitch delivered by someone with nothing to gain. Those are not the same thing, and wellness founders keep treating them like they are.

The reason video wins is specificity plus emotion. Text flattens both. "Dr. Lane changed my life" reads like filler. The same client on camera — pausing, getting a little emotional, describing the exact morning she realized her back pain was gone — that's unforgettable. The hesitation is the proof. You can't fake the hesitation.

This is why we build testimonial capture into the studio day whenever a client's model allows it. If you've already got lighting, audio, and two cameras rolling for the month's content, adding a client interview costs you twenty minutes. Trying to schedule it separately costs you the whole thing — it never happens.

The questions that get a real story (not a commercial)

The fastest way to kill a testimonial is to ask "So, why do you love working with us?" You'll get a polite, generic non-answer every time. People perform when they feel like they're performing.

Instead, we interview around the before and the turning point. The trick is to never ask them to praise you directly. Ask them to describe their experience, and the praise happens on its own.

  • "What was going on before you came in?" — This is the whole game. The problem state is what new prospects recognize themselves in.
  • "What made you finally decide to do something about it?" — Surfaces the real buying trigger, which is marketing gold.
  • "Was there a moment you knew it was working?" — Gets the specific, sensory detail that makes a story land.
  • "What would you tell someone sitting where you were a year ago?" — Lets them recommend you without you asking them to.

Notice none of those mention you by name. The client fills that in, and it reads as genuine because it is.

The consent and privacy part founders skip

This is where wellness is different from every other industry, and where I watch good founders get sloppy.

If you're a med spa, a therapist, a clinic — your clients' participation touches on health information. You cannot screenshot a text about someone's weight loss or their mental health and post it. You need explicit, written, specific consent that names where it will be used and for how long.

Get a signed release before you ever point a camera. Make it plain-language. Tell them exactly where the video goes and give them the right to pull it later. The founders who do this sleep better and, not coincidentally, get braver testimonials because clients trust the process.

And here's the part people miss: ask at the right moment. The best time to ask a client to share their story is right after they've had a win and told you about it, unprompted. That's when they want to. Waiting three months and cold-asking feels transactional to them. Catch the moment, then schedule the capture.

One client story, a month of content

A single testimonial interview is not one asset. This is the same math we run on everything we produce: one recording becomes thirty pieces. A ten-minute client conversation gives you the hero video, three or four vertical clips for social, pull-quote graphics, a written case study, and raw material for an email.

That changes the economics completely. Suddenly booking two client interviews on your monthly content day fills weeks of your calendar with the most persuasive material you'll produce all year — and it slots right into the two-week buffer so you're never scrambling.

The mistake is treating testimonials as a special project. They shouldn't be an event. They should be a standing line item in your content system, captured in the same room, the same day, with the same crew as everything else. When it's a system, you accumulate a library. When it's a project, you get one dusty video from 2022.

Where to actually use them

Don't bury your best testimonial on a page nobody visits. The highest-leverage spots we see:

  1. Mid-funnel, not just the testimonials page. Drop a client clip into the exact moment a prospect is hesitating — on the consultation booking page, in the nurture email right before the ask.
  2. As social proof inside your regular content. A client story clip performs better organically than most of your branded posts, so treat it like premium feed content, not an afterthought.
  3. In your sales conversations. "Here's someone who was exactly where you are" closes consultations.

We lean on this heavily in the client work we're proudest of — you can see how story-led content plays out across the brands we produce for. The pattern is always the same: real people, real before-and-afters, captured systematically instead of hoped for.

You already have the testimonials sitting in your client base. They want to help; most of them would be flattered you asked. What you're missing is a repeatable way to capture them on camera, with consent, and turn each one into a month of trust-building content. Build that system once and it pays you back for years.
